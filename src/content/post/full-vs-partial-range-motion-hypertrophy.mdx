Should you lower every rep all the way, or can you cut the range short and still grow? The short answer from recent research: use a full range as your default, and treat partials done in the stretched position as a valid second option — not a shortcut.

## What Full and Partial Range Actually Mean

Full range of motion means taking the joint through as much of its usable range as your mobility and the exercise allow. Think deep squat to at least parallel, dumbbell press with a full stretch at the bottom, or preacher curl from near-straight elbow to full flexion.

Partial range means slicing off part of that arc. Two versions matter:

- Lengthened partials: reps done only in the stretched half, such as the bottom half of a squat, the deep half of a push-up, or curls from straight arm to halfway up.
- Shortened partials: reps done only in the contracted half, such as the top half of a curl or lockout-only bench press.

Most of the excitement is about the first kind. The question is whether the stretch portion carries most of the growth stimulus, and whether skipping the squeezed top costs you anything.

## What the Meta-Analysis Found

The most complete synthesis so far pooled full versus partial range training across hypertrophy, strength, power, and body-composition outcomes. The headline result was a trivial-to-small edge for full range across the board, with an overall standardized effect around 0.12 in favor of full range[^1].

That number needs context. An effect of 0.12 is small enough that an individual lifter may not notice it over a single training block. It is a nudge, not a verdict. Strength results also showed specificity: people tended to gain the most strength in the range they trained, so partial training improved partial-range strength best and full training improved full-range strength best[^1].

The most interesting subgroup finding: when partials were done at long muscle lengths, they looked at least as good as full range for hypertrophy, possibly slightly better. The estimate was uncertain, with a wide interval, but it pointed away from the old idea that any partial is inferior[^1].

A later review reached a similar cautious conclusion: there is no compelling case that long-length partials beat full range outright, and the effect may differ by muscle group and exercise[^2]. In other words, full range or long-length work wins — the stretched position is the common ingredient.

## What Trained-Lifter Trials Show

Meta-analyses inherit the limits of their inputs, and many early range-of-motion studies used untrained lifters. Two newer trials in trained people deserve attention because they match how experienced lifters actually train.

In one eight-week within-participant trial, thirty trained lifters trained one arm with lengthened partials and the other with full range across upper-body exercises. Elbow flexor and extensor growth and lat-pulldown strength endurance improved similarly on both sides, with Bayesian analysis moderately favoring no difference[^3]. The practical read: emphasizing the stretch works about equally well whether you finish every rep or stay in the deep half.

A second eight-week within-subject study assigned preacher-curl arms to either full range or initial partials stopping halfway up. Mid-arm growth was nearly identical, while the partial side showed a trivial-to-small edge at the more distal measurement site. Full-range training showed a small advantage on one-repetition-max strength, with minimal absolute differences elsewhere[^4]. Again: both approaches grew muscle, with full range keeping a narrow strength-testing edge.

Neither trial supports dropping full range entirely. Together they support a simple hierarchy: train the lengthened position hard, and use full range when you can do it with good form.

## Full Range vs Lengthened Partials in Practice

Use full range as the base of your program. It trains strength across the whole arc, covers the stretch, and transfers best to full-range tests such as a one-repetition max[^1][^4]. If a lift hurts at one end of the range, shorten the range to the pain-free portion rather than pushing through joint pain.

Use lengthened partials for three jobs:

- Adding volume without extra joint stress at the lockout. The top half of some lifts loads joints while contributing less stretch stimulus.
- Keeping tension on the target muscle when fatigue breaks form. When the last inches of a row or press turn into body English, staying in the deep half preserves stimulus.
- Working around equipment limits. A machine or cable station that unloads the stretch can be paired with partials that never leave that zone.

Do not build a program on shortened partials alone. Short-range lockout work has a place for powerlifters practicing a sticking point, but for hypertrophy it discards the lengthened portion that current evidence favors. If you enjoy heavy top-half work, treat it as an accessory, not the main builder. ## How to Program Both for Growth

Keep the variables that matter most unchanged: train close to failure, progress load or reps over time, and give each muscle enough weekly sets to recover from. Range of motion is a refinement on top of that base. Our [progressive overload tracking guide](/mastering-muscle-growth-the-science-of-tracking-for-progressive-overload) covers the logging system that makes small changes like this visible.

A practical template:

- Main lift: 2-4 sets full range, 6-12 reps, leaving 1-3 reps in reserve on early sets and going closer to failure on the last set.
- Follow-up: 1-2 sets of lengthened partials on the same or a similar movement, 8-15 reps, stopping the set when you can no longer reach the deep half with control.
- Tempo: control the lowering phase at around 2 seconds so the stretch is loaded rather than bounced through. Our [rep performance guide](/optimizing-rep-performance-for-hypertrophy) details tempo prescriptions by goal.

Example pairings that work well:

- Dumbbell bench press full range, then machine chest press limited to the deep half.
- Romanian deadlift full range, then lengthened-partial leg curl or calf raise.
- Cable row full range, then lat pulldown lengthened partials after the main sets.

## Which Exercises Suit Each Approach

Single-joint moves make the cleanest test bed: curls, pushdowns, leg curls, and calf raises let you feel the stretch and stop each rep before momentum takes over. These are the lifts used in most range-of-motion trials, and they respond well to added lengthened partials as finishers.

Multi-joint lifts reward full range more because technique and transfer matter. Squats, presses, and rows build coordination across joints, and cutting them short often means cutting the hardest and most productive portion. Keep these full range, then add a machine or cable partial for extra volume if recovery allows.

Some early long-length partial findings looked promising in specific lower-leg muscles, though reviewers caution the effect may not transfer to every muscle and exercise combination[^2]. Treat that as a reason to experiment and log results, not a rule that arms and chest behave identically.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Cutting range to lift more weight is the classic error. Partial reps let you load the bar heavier, but the shorter travel distance can reduce total work and remove the stretched position that seems to drive growth. Match the range first, then pick the load.

A second mistake is turning every set into partials because they burn. The burn comes from constant tension and metabolite buildup, which feel productive but do not automatically mean more growth. Keep at least most working sets full range, and add partials deliberately.

A third mistake is chasing depth your joints do not own yet. An ass-to-grass squat with a rounded lower back or a behind-neck press with achy shoulders trades stimulus for risk. Earn depth with mobility and accessory work, and use a slightly reduced but still lengthened range in the meantime.

## The Bottom Line

Full range of motion remains the best default for muscle and strength, but the margin over lengthened partials is small[^1][^3]. If your program loads the stretched position hard — through full reps, lengthened partials, or a mix — you are covering the part of the range that current evidence says matters most.

Pick full-range lifts you can perform pain-free, progress them, add lengthened partials where they let you do more quality volume, and test strength in the range you care about.
